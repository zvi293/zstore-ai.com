Core Web Vitals, explained for business owners.

Core Web Vitals are three Google measurements of how your website feels to real visitors: how fast the main content shows up (LCP), how quickly the page reacts when someone taps (INP) and how much the layout jumps (CLS). A page passes when each one is good for at least three out of four visits: LCP within 2.5 seconds, INP within 200 milliseconds and CLS of 0.1 or less.

What are Core Web Vitals?

Core Web Vitals are the three numbers Google uses to judge what it's like to load and use a page: loading speed, responsiveness and visual stability. Google introduced them in 2020 to give site owners a short, steady list instead of dozens of speed metrics.

What makes them different from a speed test is where the numbers come from. They're collected from real people using Chrome, on their own phones and connections, and summarized over the last 28 days. So they describe what your actual visitors went through, not what your laptop sees on office Wi-Fi. Each one answers a question your visitors would ask if they could:

What do LCP, INP and CLS measure?

LCP times the main content, INP times the reaction to taps and clicks, and CLS scores how far things move without warning. Here's each one in a sentence or three.

LCP: Largest Contentful Paint

LCP is the moment the biggest image or block of text in the first screen finishes appearing. On most business sites that's the hero photo or the main headline. It's a good stand-in for “the page has loaded” from a visitor's point of view: once the main thing is there, people start reading, even if smaller parts are still arriving. Slow servers, oversized images and code that stops the page from drawing are the usual reasons it's late.

INP: Interaction to Next Paint

INP watches the taps, clicks and key presses during a visit and measures how long the page takes to show a visible response, like a menu opening or a button changing. It reports roughly the slowest of them, so one sluggish “Add to cart” can set the score. INP replaced First Input Delay as a Core Web Vital in March 2024, because it covers the whole visit, not just the first tap.

CLS: Cumulative Layout Shift

CLS adds up how much visible content moves unexpectedly: text that drops when an image arrives, a banner that pushes the page down, a button that slides away just as you reach for it. Movement right after you tap, like an accordion opening, doesn't count. The score has no units. It's the share of the screen that moved multiplied by how far it moved, taken from the worst burst of shifts during the visit.

What does a slow, jumpy page feel like?

Like trying to tap a button that keeps running away from your thumb. Try it: load the same example page as a slow site, then as a tuned one, and tap Order ahead the moment you see it. The gauges score each run as it happens.

Nothing in the slow version is unusual. A photo without set dimensions, a widget that loads late, a web font that swaps in, a few third-party scripts busy at the wrong moment: most sites have at least one of these. The tuned version loads the very same content. It reserves space for everything and keeps the main thread free for the visitor.

What scores count as good?

Good means LCP of 2.5 seconds or less, INP of 200 milliseconds or less and CLS of 0.1 or less, measured at the 75th percentile of visits. Mobile and desktop are assessed separately, and a page passes only when all three are good.

The 75th percentile is the part most people miss. Google doesn't average your visits. It lines them up from fastest to slowest and reads the value three quarters of the way along. So a page passes only when at least 75% of visits are good, which stops a slow quarter of your audience from hiding behind a fast majority. Watch it happen with twenty example visits:

Do Core Web Vitals affect Google rankings?

Yes, a little. Google uses them as part of its page experience signals, but relevance and the quality of your content matter far more.

Google's own documentation recommends good Core Web Vitals for success in Search and says they line up with what its core ranking systems reward. The same documentation is clear that a great page experience doesn't override having great, relevant content. In practice, they work closer to a tie-breaker between pages that answer a search equally well than a shortcut to the top.

The bigger reason to care is the people behind the numbers. A visitor staring at a blank screen, tapping the wrong button or watching a form lag isn't thinking about rankings. They're deciding whether to stay. Lean pages that load fast are also quicker for crawlers to fetch, including the ones behind AI search engines, which is one more reason I treat speed as part of the build rather than an extra.

How do I check my Core Web Vitals?

Paste a page's address into Google's free PageSpeed Insights, then open the Core Web Vitals report in Search Console. Together they show what real visitors experience and which pages need work. Four steps, in the order I'd take them:

  1. 01

    Read the field data first

    Go to pagespeed.web.dev and enter a page. The top section, which describes what your real users are experiencing, is field data from the Chrome UX Report: real visits over the last 28 days. That's the part that decides pass or fail.

    Field data · Free
  2. 02

    Use the lab test for clues

    Below it, Lighthouse runs a one-off test on a simulated phone and lists what slowed it down. Its 0–100 performance score is a lab grade, not a Core Web Vitals result, so read it for causes, not for a verdict.

    Lab data · Diagnosis
  3. 03

    See the whole site in Search Console

    In Search Console, open Core Web Vitals under Experience. It groups similar pages, splits mobile from desktop and shows which groups are poor, need improvement or are good. After a fix, press Validate fix and give it about four weeks of fresh data.

    Field data · Every page
  4. 04

    Use it like a customer

    Open the site on an ordinary phone over mobile data. Tap the menu, the main button and the form. If something feels slow or jumps, it will show up in the field data sooner or later.

    Reality check

Tip

Test on a mid-range Android phone on mobile data, not on the fast laptop the site was built on. Most visitors don't have that laptop, and at the 75th percentile, the slower phones are the ones that decide your score.

What usually fixes a poor score?

Most poor scores come from a few causes: oversized images, too much JavaScript and space that isn't reserved. Fix them in the template rather than page by page, and every page built on it improves at once.

LCPGet the main content on screen sooner

  • Serve the hero image in a modern format like WebP or AVIF, sized for phones, never straight from the camera.
  • Don't lazy-load the hero image. Mark it as high priority so the browser fetches it first.
  • Cut what blocks the first paint: unused CSS, extra font files, scripts loaded at the top of the page.
  • Use hosting with good caching and a CDN, so the first byte arrives quickly wherever visitors are.

INPKeep the page free to respond

  • Remove third-party scripts you no longer use: old trackers, chat widgets, testing tools.
  • Load the ones you keep after the page is usable, not before.
  • Break long JavaScript tasks into smaller pieces, so the browser can respond in between.
  • Show feedback the instant someone taps, such as a pressed state or a spinner, then do the work.

CLSReserve the space, then fill it

  • Give every image, video and embed a width and height, or an aspect ratio, so its space is held while it loads.
  • Reserve room for banners, ads and widgets that arrive late, or show them over the content instead of inside it.
  • Pick a fallback font with similar proportions, so text barely moves when the web font swaps in.
  • Animate with transforms, not by changing an element's size or position in the layout.

On page builders and heavy themes, some of these fixes are out of your hands, because the platform decides which scripts and styles every page loads. That's one of the honest trade-offs in choosing between a custom website and a website builder.

When is it worth getting help?

When the field data stays poor after the easy fixes, or when the causes live in the theme, the platform or scripts you can't remove yourself.

Compressing images and removing a plugin you don't need are jobs many owners can do. Render-blocking code, long JavaScript tasks and layouts that shift inside a template usually need someone who can change the code. That's the kind of work I do in a performance pass, and it's built into everything I make from scratch: my hand-coded projects load in about 1.2 seconds on average, because each page ships only what it needs. Why does my PageSpeed Insights score change every time I run it?

The 0–100 score comes from a single lab test, and small differences in server response, network conditions and third-party scripts move it from run to run. The field data at the top of the report is much steadier, because it summarizes 28 days of real visits. Judge progress by that, and by trends rather than one run.

Is the Lighthouse performance score the same as Core Web Vitals?

No. Lighthouse runs a simulated test and blends several lab metrics into a score out of 100. Core Web Vitals come from real visitors' Chrome browsers. A page can score in the 90s in the lab and still fail in the field, or the other way around, because real phones and networks vary so much.

Why does Search Console say there isn't enough data?

Chrome only reports a page, or a group of similar pages, once enough real visits have been recorded. New and smaller sites often don't reach that bar yet. Until they do, use the lab test in PageSpeed Insights and your own phone, and fix what they show. The field data follows as traffic grows.

Do I need a perfect score of 100?

No. Core Web Vitals pass or fail on thresholds, so a page that's comfortably inside all three good ranges gains nothing extra in Google's assessment by going further. Chasing the last few lab points can cost more than it's worth. Get your key pages to good, keep them there, and put the rest of the effort into content.

What happened to First Input Delay (FID)?

Google replaced it with INP as a Core Web Vital in March 2024. FID measured only the delay before the browser started handling the first interaction, so most sites passed it easily. INP measures the full time to a visible response for interactions throughout the visit, which is much closer to what people actually feel.
