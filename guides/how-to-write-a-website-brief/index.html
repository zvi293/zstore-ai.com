How to write a website brief (with a template you can copy).

To write a website brief, explain why the site exists, who it's for, what it must do, what content you already have, and your timeline and constraints, in one or two pages. You don't need design vocabulary: clear goals and honest answers matter more. Below are the nine sections to include, the questions behind each, and a template you can fill in and send.

Why does a website brief matter?

A brief turns a vague idea into something a designer can scope, quote and plan. Without one, the first weeks of a project go into discovering things you already knew, and every proposal you receive is built on a different guess.

It doesn't need to be polished. It needs to be honest about four things: what the site is for, who it's for, what you already have, and what has to be true by launch day. When a brief like that lands in my inbox, I can reply within one business day with sharp questions or a first plan, instead of a list of basics.

  • 01Quotes you can compare. When every designer reads the same goals and scope, their proposals differ on approach and timeline, not on what they imagined you meant.
  • 02Fewer surprises mid-project. Most late changes come from something that was known but never written down: a booking tool, a second language, a launch date tied to an event.
  • 03A faster start. A designer who understands the business on day one spends the first conversation on ideas instead of intake questions.
  • 04Better decisions on your side. Writing it forces the choices that would otherwise stall the project later: who signs off, what matters most, what can wait for phase two.

What should a website brief include?

Nine sections cover everything a designer needs: the business, your goals, the audience, the one main action, scope, content, look and feel, technical details, and timeline, budget and sign-off. Each takes a few sentences. Answer what you can; a blank section is still useful, because it shows where you want help.

  1. 01

    About the business

    The context a stranger needs before anything else makes sense.

    • What do you do, and how does the business make money?
    • What makes you different from the alternatives a customer would also look at?
    • Is anything about to change: a new service, a new market, a rebrand?
  2. 02

    Goals

    What the site has to achieve, and how you'll know it did.

    • What must the website achieve in its first year?
    • How will you know it worked: more calls, bookings, sales, fewer repetitive emails?
    • If you have a site today, what is it failing to do?
  3. 03

    Audience

    The people the site is really for, in your words, not a persona template.

    • Who are the two or three kinds of visitors that matter most?
    • What do they need to know before they act?
    • What do they usually worry about or push back on?
  4. 04

    The one main action

    The single thing most visitors should do. It becomes the site's main call to action.

    • Should most visitors book, buy, call, request a quote or sign up? Pick one.
    • What happens right after they do it, and who handles it?
  5. 05

    Scope

    A rough list is enough. The designer turns it into the site's structure, its information architecture.

    • Which pages or sections do you imagine?
    • Which features do you need: booking, a store, forms, a blog, more than one language?
    • Do you want to edit content yourself? Then say which parts and how often, so the right CMS gets planned in.
  6. 06

    Content

    Words and pictures shape the design more than anything else, so be specific about what exists.

    • What exists already: copy, photos, logo, video, product data, reviews you're allowed to use?
    • What still needs writing or shooting, and who will do it?
    • When will it be ready?
  7. 07

    Look and feel

    Examples beat adjectives every time.

    • Which three websites do you like, and what exactly do you like about each one?
    • What brand assets exist: logo, colors, fonts, guidelines?
    • What do you want to avoid?
  8. 08

    Technical details

    The unglamorous facts that decide how smooth the launch is.

    • Is there a current site, and what is it built on?
    • Who controls the domain name, the hosting and the logins?
    • Which tools must the site connect to: booking, email marketing, a CRM, payments, analytics?
  9. 09

    Timeline, budget and sign-off

    The constraints that shape the proposal. Honest ones save everyone time.

    • Is there a real deadline, and what's behind it?
    • What budget range or comfort level should the proposal fit? It tells the designer whether to propose a focused first version or a full build.
    • Who makes the final decisions, and who else needs a say?

Tip

Record a five-minute voice note answering the nine questions, then tidy up the transcript. Most people say more, and say it more clearly, than they would ever write.

Can you write a website brief in one paragraph?

For a first draft, yes. One honest paragraph with the business, the goal, the main action, your content and a real deadline gives a designer enough to ask the right questions. Tap each blank to build yours; the full template follows if you want to go deeper.

What does a good website brief look like?

Short, specific and goal-first. Here is an example for a fictional business, a family-run bike repair shop, filled in the way I'd love to receive one. Notice that no answer is long, and none of them uses design vocabulary.

Readyto quote ≈ one page

Example briefFor a fictional business

01Business
A family-run bike repair shop. We earn from repairs and servicing, plus a small shelf of parts. Customers tell us we explain the problem before we fix it.
02Goals
Get most repairs booked online instead of by phone, so we stop missing calls while our hands are greasy. It worked if the phone rings less and the calendar still fills.
03Audience
Commuters who need their bike back fast, and weekend riders who want a proper service. Both worry about how long they'll be without a bike.
04Main action
Book a repair slot in about a minute, from a phone.
05Scope
Home, Repairs and services, Book a repair, About, Contact. Online booking that syncs with the calendar we already use. We want to edit opening hours and seasonal notices ourselves.
06Content
Good workshop photos and service descriptions from the old site we can reuse. No About text yet; we'd like help writing it.
07Look and feel
Friendly, quick, local. We like a neighborhood café's site for its warmth and a transit app for how fast it is. No stock photos of models on bikes.
08Technical
The current site is a builder template. We own the domain and have the logins. The booking tool must sync with our shared calendar.
09Timeline and sign-off
Live by the start of spring, our busiest season. We'd rather launch a focused first version and add an online shop later. Both owners sign off, and we reply within a day.

That's about a page. A designer reading it knows the goal, the audience, the one action, the content gaps and the real deadline, and can come back with a proposal instead of a questionnaire. What mistakes make a brief less useful?

Vague words, missing goals and hidden constraints. Each one forces the designer to guess, and every guess turns into a revision later. Here are the five I see most, with the rewrite that fixes each one.

  1. Vague adjectives without examples

    Instead of“We want it modern, clean and pop.”

    Write“Calm and premium, like the two sites linked below: big photos, short paragraphs, nothing dark.”

    Everyone's “modern” is different. Two links and one sentence on why make it concrete.

  2. A page list with no goals

    Instead of“Pages: Home, About, Services, Blog, Contact.”

    Write“Goal: more booked consultations. Visitors need to see what we do, trust us, then book.”

    Pages follow from goals. A list without one leaves the designer arranging rooms with no idea who lives there.

  3. Hiding the real deadline

    Instead of“No rush, whenever it's ready.”

    Write“We're at a trade show in mid-March, so the site should be live a week before.”

    A deadline you keep to yourself still arrives. Said up front, it shapes a realistic plan.

  4. No decision-maker named

    Instead of“We'll decide as a team.”

    Write“Dana approves the design, Sam signs off on scope, and both reply within two days.”

    Projects rarely stall on design. They stall waiting for an answer nobody owns.

  5. Asking for everything in phase one

    Instead of“A store, a blog, a members area, booking and three languages.”

    Write“Phase one: services and booking in English. The store and a second language come next.”

    A focused first version launches sooner and teaches you what phase two really needs.

How long should a website brief be?

One or two pages is usually enough. The goal is clarity, not volume: a designer should understand the business, the goals and the scope in a few minutes. Link to examples and existing materials rather than pasting everything into the brief.

Should I include a budget in my brief?

It helps, even as a rough comfort level. A budget tells the designer which scope makes sense, for example a focused first version or a full build, so the proposal fits your reality instead of a guess. You can also ask for options at different scopes.

What if I don't know what pages my website needs?

That's fine. Describe what visitors need to learn and do, and the designer can propose the structure. “People need to see my services, trust me, and book a call” is more useful than a page list you're unsure about.

Is a brief the same as a proposal?

No. You write the brief to explain what you need; the designer replies with a proposal that sets out the scope, deliverables, timeline and price. A clear brief makes the proposal faster to write and more accurate.

Should I send the same brief to several designers?

Yes, if you're comparing. The same brief makes proposals easier to compare on approach, scope and timeline, not just on the final figure. Ask each designer what they would change or question in your brief; the answer tells you a lot.
