05Guide · Accessibility

Website accessibility and the ADA, in plain English.

In the US, the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is widely applied to business websites, even though there's still no specific federal web rule for private companies. Courts and settlements usually point to WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the benchmark. The practical answer: build and fix your site to WCAG AA, document your efforts, and ask a qualified attorney about your specific legal risk.

Does the ADA apply to websites?

The Department of Justice says the ADA applies to the web content of businesses open to the public, but there is still no detailed federal web rule for private businesses. So the obligation is real, while the exact technical requirements come from practice rather than a regulation.

The ADA is a 1990 civil-rights law that bans discrimination on the basis of disability. Its Title III covers “public accommodations”: privately owned businesses that serve the public, such as shops, restaurants, hotels, gyms, banks, dental and medical practices, law firms and many more. The law was written before websites mattered, but the DOJ's guidance (most recently in 2022) says businesses open to the public need to make their websites accessible to people with disabilities. It leaves the “how” fairly open.

  • IITitle II

    Public entities

    Cities, counties, public schools and universities, transit agencies, courts and libraries.

    A web rule since 2024: WCAG 2.1 AA.

  • IIITitle III

    Businesses open to the public

    Shops, restaurants, hotels, gyms, banks, dental and medical practices, law firms.

    Most businesses

In practice, nobody inspects your site. Enforcement happens through private lawsuits, demand letters and, occasionally, DOJ action. Courts in different parts of the country have disagreed on details, for example whether a website has to be connected to a physical location to be covered, so the risk depends partly on where you and your customers are.

The sensible reading for a business owner: if your website is how people learn about you, book, buy or get in touch, treat it as part of serving the public. The problems that cause real trouble are the ones a visitor can't work around:

  • a contact or booking form that can't be completed with a keyboard or a screen reader,
  • a checkout with unlabeled fields, or buttons that are only pictures,
  • important images and documents with no text alternative,
  • videos without captions, and text too faint to read.

What changed with the DOJ's 2024 rule?

It made WCAG 2.1 Level AA the legal standard for state and local government websites and apps under Title II of the ADA. It doesn't cover private businesses. Title II is the part of the law for public entities: cities, counties, public schools and universities, transit agencies, courts and libraries.

The DOJ published the rule in April 2024 with two compliance dates based on population size. In April 2026 an interim final rule moved both dates back by one year, and kept the technical standard the same: April 26, 2027 for entities serving 50,000 or more people, and April 26, 2028 for smaller entities and special districts. The DOJ has also said it may revisit the rule, so check the current dates at ada.gov before you plan around them.

For private businesses

Why should a private business care? Because it's the clearest statement yet of the technical standard the federal government considers reasonable for the web. Title III cases and settlements were already pointing to WCAG 2.1 AA; the Title II rule makes that benchmark harder to argue with. And if you sell to, or build for, public bodies, expect them to ask their suppliers for accessible content.

  2. April 24, 2024The DOJ's Title II web rule is published: WCAG 2.1 AA for state and local governments.

What does WCAG 2.1 / 2.2 Level AA actually require?

WCAG is a set of testable success criteria organized under four principles: perceivable, operable, understandable and robust. Level AA is the middle of its three levels (A, AA, AAA), and it's the one laws, courts and procurement teams usually point to.

WCAG, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, is published by the W3C, the body that also maintains web standards like HTML and CSS. It doesn't prescribe a look or a technology. Each criterion describes an outcome you can test, such as “all functionality is available from a keyboard.” Here's what the four principles mean on an ordinary business site:

  • P

    Perceivable

    People can take in the content with the senses they use. Images that carry meaning have alt text, videos have captions, and normal text has a color contrast of at least 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text).

  • O

    Operable

    Everything works with a keyboard, with a visible focus indicator and no keyboard traps. Nothing flashes more than three times a second, and moving content can be paused.

  • U

    Understandable

    Forms have visible labels and clear error messages that say how to fix the problem. Navigation stays consistent from page to page, and the page language is set.

  • R

    Robust

    The code is semantic, so assistive technology can read it: real headings, landmarks, buttons and form controls, each with the right name, role and state.

Two WCAG 2.1 criteria matter a lot on phones. Reflow asks that content works at a width of 320 CSS pixels without scrolling sideways, and Resize text asks that text can be zoomed to 200% without breaking the page. A responsive site built with care meets both almost by default.

WCAG 2.2, published in October 2023, adds a handful of criteria. A few you'll notice in practice: Focus Not Obscured (a sticky header or cookie banner can't completely cover the element that has focus), Target Size (Minimum) (controls at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels, with exceptions), Dragging Movements (anything you drag can also be done with a simple tap or click) and Accessible Authentication (no memory puzzles to log in). Meeting 2.2 AA generally satisfies 2.1 AA as well, so for new work, aim at 2.2 AA.

What does keyboard-only browsing feel like?

Like reading a page through a keyhole: one element at a time, in the order the code sets, and only the parts the code lets you reach. People who can't use a mouse move with Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space and the arrow keys, or with switches and voice control that act the same way. Screen reader users travel a similar path. Try it on an example page below.

Every failure in the broken run comes from ordinary code: a focus style someone removed, a <div> standing in for a button, a carousel script that catches focus, a banner left out of the tab order. None of it needs a redesign to fix, which is why a keyboard test is the cheapest accessibility check you can run.

Do accessibility overlays or widgets make a site compliant?

No. No tool can make an inaccessible site compliant on its own. Overlays are scripts you add with one line of code. They put a toolbar on the page and try to repair problems automatically as the page loads.

The trouble is that the real problems live in the code, and a script running on top of it can only guess. It can't know why an image is on the page, so its generated descriptions are often wrong. It can't turn a checkout built from plain <div> elements into real form controls, or untangle a keyboard trap that another script creates. And many screen reader users already have their own tools set up, which an overlay can get in the way of.

Many disability advocates and accessibility professionals have publicly criticized overlays, and businesses using them have still been sued. In 2025 the US Federal Trade Commission finalized an order against one overlay vendor, accessiBe, that bars the company from claiming its automated tool can make any website comply with WCAG.

What should I do first?

Find out where your site stands, fix the shared parts first, and make accessibility part of how the site is maintained. Six steps, in the order I'd take them:

  1. 01

    Run an automated check

    Free tools like WAVE or Lighthouse find issues such as missing alt text, low contrast and empty links. They catch only part of the problems, so treat the result as a starting list.

    Free tools
  2. 02

    Put the mouse away

    Use Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter and the arrow keys to contact, book or buy on your own site. Watch for invisible focus, traps and skipped buttons.

    Your key journeys
  3. 03

    Try a screen reader

    VoiceOver is built into iPhone and Mac, TalkBack into Android, and NVDA is free on Windows. Listen to whether headings, links and buttons make sense out of context.

    Built into most devices
  4. 04

    Fix templates first

    Header, menu, forms, product cards, footer. One fix in a shared component repairs every page that uses it.

    One fix, many pages
  5. 05

    Publish a statement

    Say which standard you aim for, what you know isn't there yet and how people can reach you, like the statement on this site.

    A route to you
  6. 06

    Keep it in the routine

    Alt text when you upload an image, captions when you post a video, a keyboard check when you add a page. My launch checklist includes these checks.

    Every update

Does accessibility law apply outside the US?

Yes. The EU, the UK and Canada all have accessibility laws that can cover websites, and some of them reach businesses based elsewhere. If you sell to customers in those places, their rules may apply to you, wherever your company sits.

The biggest recent change is the European Accessibility Act. Since June 28, 2025, it has applied to a defined list of products and services sold to consumers in the EU, including e-commerce, consumer banking and e-books. That can include a store based in the US or anywhere else that sells to EU customers. Very small service businesses (microenterprises) are exempt from its service requirements. Its technical reference is the European standard EN 301 549, which incorporates WCAG for web content.

Is accessibility only about legal risk?

No. Mostly it decides how many people can actually use your site, and the same work makes the site clearer for everyone. The World Health Organization estimates that about 1.3 billion people live with a significant disability.

And disability isn't the only reason someone needs an accessible page. Think of an older customer who zooms the text, someone with a broken wrist using one hand, a phone screen in bright sun, or a video watched on a quiet train with the sound off. Captions, contrast, large tap targets and keyboard support help all of them.

There's a quieter benefit too. Real headings, labeled forms, descriptive links and text alternatives are exactly what search engines and AI tools read to understand a page. The structure that lets a screen reader make sense of your site is the same structure that helps it get found and quoted, which I explain in the guide to generative engine optimization.

Is there an official ADA certification for websites?

No. There's no government certification that marks a website “ADA compliant.” Vendors may offer badges or certificates, but they don't carry legal weight. What counts in practice is how accessible the site actually is, usually measured against WCAG 2.1 or 2.2 Level AA, and evidence of ongoing effort.

Does a small business website need to be accessible?

If your business serves the public, it's wise to assume yes. Title III of the ADA has no small-business exemption for public accommodations, and lawsuits have targeted businesses of all sizes. Beyond the legal question, an accessible site works better for every visitor. A qualified attorney can assess your specific risk.

How do I know if my website meets WCAG AA?

Combine automated scans, which catch some issues like missing alt text and low contrast, with manual testing by keyboard and screen reader, ideally by someone experienced in accessibility. A full WCAG AA audit checks each criterion on representative pages and documents what passes and what needs fixing.

What is an accessibility statement?

A page that explains your commitment to accessibility, the standard you aim for (such as WCAG 2.2 AA), any known limitations, and how people can report a problem or get help another way. It shows good faith and gives users a route to you before frustration turns into a complaint.

Do PDFs and videos on my site need to be accessible too?

Yes, they're part of your web content. PDFs need real text, headings, tags and reading order; videos need captions, and audio-only content needs transcripts. Often the simplest fix is publishing the information as a normal web page instead of a PDF.
