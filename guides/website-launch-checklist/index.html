04Guide · Launch & growth

The website launch checklist: before, on the day, and after.

A website launch checklist works best in three phases. Before launch, check content, forms, speed, accessibility, SEO basics and redirects on a private copy of the site. On launch day, switch the domain, confirm HTTPS, re-test the key journeys and submit your sitemap. In the first 30 days, watch for errors, broken links and search coverage, and fix what real visitors reveal.

What should I check before launching a website?

Check everything a visitor, a search engine and you will rely on, on a private copy of the site, before anyone else sees it. That means content, forms, phones, speed, accessibility, search basics, AI search access, analytics and who holds the keys. I group the work into nine areas so nothing hides in one long list, and each area maps to one switch on the console above.

Do this pass a few days before your launch date, not the evening before. Most fixes are small, but you want time to make them calmly.

1. Content

Read every page as a stranger would. Lorem ipsum has a way of surviving in footers, image captions and confirmation emails, so search the whole site for it. Check spelling, dates and names, and make sure your phone number, email and address are identical everywhere they appear. Legal pages (privacy policy, terms, any cookie notice) should exist and describe the site you're actually launching.

2. Forms and function

  • Every form sends to an inbox someone reads, shows a clear confirmation, and the auto-reply (if you have one) arrives.
  • Every link goes somewhere real, including the footer and the logo. A link checker catches what your eyes skip.
  • The 404 page exists, looks like your site and offers a way back.
  • Payments pass end to end in test mode first. Then run one small real transaction on launch day and refund it.

3. Phones and browsers

Most visitors will meet your site on a phone, so test on real ones: an iPhone and an Android at least, in Safari and Chrome. A narrowed desktop window doesn't show you the on-screen keyboard covering a form field, a menu that's hard to close with a thumb, or text that's too small to read in sunlight. Check the main desktop browsers too.

4. Speed

Make sure images are sized for the space they fill and compressed (a modern format like WebP helps), fonts are limited to what you use, and nothing heavy loads that the page doesn't need. Then run a lab test such as PageSpeed Insights on your key pages and look at the Core Web Vitals: how fast the main content appears, how quickly the page responds to a tap, and whether things jump around while it loads. Lab scores are a preview; the real numbers come from visitors after launch. My guide to Core Web Vitals explains what each one measures and what usually fixes it.

5. Accessibility

Put the mouse away and use the site with the Tab key: can you reach every link and button, and can you always see where you are? Check that images have meaningful alt text, text has enough contrast against its background, and headings follow a logical order. These accessibility checks take an hour on a small site and they make it better for everyone, not only for people who use assistive technology.

6. SEO basics

  • A unique title and meta description on every page, written for people, with the page's main topic near the start.
  • One H1 per page, and headings that describe the sections below them.
  • Canonical tags pointing at the one address you want indexed for each page.
  • An XML sitemap listing every page you want found, and nothing you don't.
  • A robots.txt that doesn't block the site. Many sites go live with a “block everything” rule left over from development, and it can quietly keep a site out of search for weeks.
  • Structured data (if you use it) that passes Google's Rich Results Test without errors.

7. AI search

AI assistants and answer engines run their own crawlers, and your robots.txt can allow or block each of them separately. Decide on purpose, rather than inheriting a default. Then look at your key pages: does each one open with a clear, direct answer to the question it exists for? That is what gets quoted. If this part matters to your business, it's the core of my AI search optimization work.

8. Analytics and privacy

Install analytics and confirm that it records a visit (open the site on your phone and watch it appear). If you serve visitors in places that require consent for tracking, the consent banner must work before any tracking fires. Read your privacy policy once more: it should match what the site actually collects, including form data and any third-party tools.

9. Backups and access

Take a full backup of the old site (if there is one) and the new one. Then write down who controls the domain registrar, the DNS, the hosting and the email. The domain should be registered in your business's name, with logins you can reach. Launch day is the worst moment to discover that the only person with the DNS password left the company.

What happens on launch day?

On launch day you point the domain at the new site, confirm it loads securely at one address, re-test the things that make money, open the doors to search engines and only then tell people. The order matters: each step checks the one before it. Here is the run sheet, with where each step happens:

Launch dayWhere

  1. 01Lower the DNS TTL a day ahead. A short time-to-live means the change spreads in minutes instead of hours.DNS
  2. 02Point the domain. Update the DNS records to the new host and wait for the new site to answer.DNS
  3. 03Confirm HTTPS and one canonical host. The padlock shows, and the www and non-www, http and https versions all redirect to the same address.Browser
  4. 04Re-test forms and checkout live. Send a real form and place a real order, then refund it.Live site
  5. 05Remove any noindex and check robots.txt. Make sure nothing from development still tells search engines to stay away.Robots.txt
  6. 06Submit the sitemap. Add it in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools.Search tools
  7. 07Request indexing and ping IndexNow. Inspect your most important pages and ask for indexing; IndexNow tells Bing and other participating engines straight away.URL inspect
  8. 08Tell people. Customers, social media and your email list, once everything above holds.Inbox

A few notes on the steps that confuse people most.

01DNS · TTLDNS is the address book that tells browsers where your domain lives. Each record carries a “time-to-live,” which is how long other servers may remember the old answer. Lowering it the day before means that when you change the record, the world catches up quickly, and if you have to roll back, that's quick too.

02One hostOne canonical host means your site answers at exactly one address. Pick either www or the bare domain, and redirect everything else to it with a permanent redirect, so search engines don't see four copies of every page.

03IndexingGoogle Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools are free. Verify your domain in both before launch day so you can submit the sitemap the moment the site is live. IndexNow is a simple protocol that lets your site notify participating search engines about new or changed URLs; Google doesn't use it, which is why the Search Console steps still matter.

Tip

Launch on a Tuesday morning, not a Friday afternoon: you want a few working days to catch anything odd.

What should I do in the first 30 days after launch?

Watch, fix and listen. For the first month, check how search engines are picking up the site, fix the broken links and errors real traffic uncovers, read every form submission, and ask a few customers what they think. Most of what you'll find is small. Here is how the month usually unfolds, one page at a time:

  1. Day1Stay close. Watch the form inbox and any error alerts. Fix anything that blocks a visitor before you fix anything that only looks wrong.
  2. Days2–3Crawl the live site. Run a link checker for broken links and redirect loops, and look at which URLs are hitting your 404 page.
  3. Week1Open Search Console. Check the sitemap report and the page indexing report. Are your key pages discovered? Indexed? If not, the report usually says why.
  4. Week2Ask real people. Have three customers use the site for something they'd actually do, and notice where they pause or get lost.
  5. Week3Read the numbers. Look at analytics for odd drops and at Core Web Vitals field data as it starts to arrive from real visitors.
  6. Day30Ship and write down. Release the batch of small fixes, note the bigger ideas for later, and set a monthly check-in with yourself.

Core Web Vitals field data comes from real Chrome users, so a new or low-traffic site may show nothing for a while. That's normal; keep using lab tests until it appears. In Search Console, don't panic about pages listed as “discovered” or “crawled, currently not indexed” in the first weeks. Look for patterns instead: a whole section missing usually points to a technical cause, like a stray noindex or a canonical tag pointing at the wrong page.

30days of careThis is also why every project I build includes 30 days of post-launch care: real visitors always find something testing didn't, and it's better to fix it together while the site is fresh. You can see where that fits in the launch step of my process.

What extra steps does a relaunch or redesign need?

A relaunch needs everything above plus a plan to keep what the old site earned: its URLs, its rankings and its links from other sites. The tool for that is a redirect map, built and tested before launch day.

  1. Inventory the old URLs. Export every URL from the old sitemap, a crawl, and your analytics' most-visited pages. Pages with backlinks matter most.
  2. Map each one to its best new home. Every old URL gets a 301 redirect to the closest matching new page. Send it to the homepage only when nothing related exists.
  3. Avoid chains. Redirect old URLs straight to their final address. A redirect that hops through two or three others is slower and harder for search engines to follow.
  4. Keep what ranks. For pages that bring traffic today, keep the topic, the intent and ideally the title close to what worked. A redesign is a good time to improve a page, not to change what it's about.
  5. Test the map before launch. Run the old URL list against the new site and check that each one lands where you planned, in one hop.
  6. Keep a baseline. Note traffic, top pages and conversions before launch, so you can compare honestly after it.

    Launch questions.

    5 straight answers

    Planning a launch and stuck on something else?

    Ask me directly →
    How long does it take for a new website to appear on Google?

    It varies from days to weeks. Submitting your XML sitemap and requesting indexing in Search Console helps Google discover pages sooner, but indexing isn't guaranteed or instant. New domains often take longer than relaunches of established ones.

    Should I take my old site down before the new one is ready?

    No. Keep the old site live until the new one is fully tested, then switch over in one move. Taking it down early loses visitors and search signals, and leaves customers with a broken experience in between.

    Do I need a “coming soon” page before launch?

    Only if it does a job. A simple page that explains what's coming and collects emails can be useful for a new brand. For an existing business, keep your current site up until the new one launches instead.

    What's a soft launch?

    Launching quietly, without announcements, so real visitors use the site for a few days before you promote it. It's a calm way to catch issues that testing missed. Afterward you announce it to customers, on social media and by email.

    What should I do if something breaks right after launch?

    Fix the highest-impact issue first, usually forms, checkout or broken key pages. If the problem is serious and can't be fixed quickly, rolling back to the previous version or putting up a clear temporary notice is better than leaving a broken experience live.
