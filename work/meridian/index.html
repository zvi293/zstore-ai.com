01The brief I gave myself

What question did this study start with?

What does a busy owner need to know in five seconds?

Most finance dashboards show everything they can measure. Owners open them between two other jobs, with one question in mind, and leave with eight numbers and no answer. I wanted the opposite: a dashboard built for the at-a-glance moment, where every number, card and chart has a clear place in the hierarchy.

The three questions I designed around

  1. Q1Can I pay everything that's due this month?
  2. Q2Who owes me, and how late are they?
  3. Q3Is this month better or worse than last?

How do you design a finance dashboard UI people can read at a glance?

Decide what the one answer is, make it the loudest thing on screen, and give everything else a quieter, predictable place. These are the rules Meridian follows.

  1. 01

    One primary metric per view

    Each screen answers one main question with one number. Everything else supports it, smaller and quieter, so your eye lands in the same place every morning.

  2. 02

    Group by question, not by data source

    Bank feeds, invoices and payroll come from different systems. Owners don't think that way, so cards are grouped by the question they answer: what's due, what's coming in, how we're doing.

  3. 03

    Consistent number formatting

    Same decimals, same alignment, same sign for money going out. Right-aligned tabular figures let you compare a column without reading every digit.

  4. 04

    Color for meaning, not decoration

    Lavender is the calm ground. Color changes only when it means something, like an overdue invoice, and it never works alone: a word or an icon always says it too.

  5. 05

    Charts chosen for the comparison

    Bars to compare months, a line for a trend, a single number when there's nothing to compare. If a chart needs a legend before it can be read, it's probably the wrong chart.

06

Design the states nobody screenshots.

A new account with no data yet, a slow bank sync, a connection that fails. Empty, loading and error states are designed with the same care as the happy path, because that's where trust is won or lost.

03Beyond the study

What does this study show about product design?

The discipline I bring to websites carries straight over to dashboards and web apps: start from the task, then design every screen and every state around it.

Meridian is a small sample of the digital product design work I take on: user journeys, interface design, interactive prototypes and design systems. Good UI design for a product has to survive daily, repeated use, not just a first impression.

  • User journeys mapped before any screen is drawn
  • Clickable prototypes tested with real tasks
  • A design system, so screen thirty matches screen one
  • Front-end code when the design needs to ship

What did I learn from it?

Hierarchy is subtraction.

My first sketches kept adding things: a smarter chart, a better alert, one more helpful card. Each was reasonable on its own, and together they pushed the answer back into the crowd. The design only started working when I began removing, and asking of every element: does this help answer the five-second question, or can it wait one tap?

Nothing was deleted from the product. Payroll, tax and every transaction are still there, one tap away. They just stopped competing for the first look.

Something else on your mind?

Just ask me →
Are the numbers in Meridian real?

No. Every balance, chart and transaction in Meridian is illustrative sample data made up for this design study. There is no company, bank or client behind it. The point is the hierarchy and interaction design, not the figures.

Can you design dashboards and web apps, not just websites?

Yes. Digital product design, from user journeys and interface design to interactive prototypes and design systems, is part of my work. Dashboards are a good example: they need the same clarity as a marketing site, plus careful handling of data, states and repeated use.

What's the difference between a dashboard and a report?

A report answers a question once; a dashboard answers the same questions every day at a glance. That's why dashboards need a stable layout, one clear primary metric and fast scanning, while reports can afford longer explanations and more detail.

How do you test whether a dashboard design works?

With a clickable prototype and real tasks. I ask people who match the audience to find specific answers, like “how much did you spend on suppliers last month?”, and watch where they look first and where they hesitate. The hierarchy gets adjusted until the answers are found quickly.
